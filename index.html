Roles from the Group Roles Report E3

Lift Right

A first-person 360° simulation that runs in a normal browser. A warehouse worker walks a virtual warehouse and is scored in real time on the safety decisions made during a lift.

Why it is needed E8

Problem with the current approachHow Lift Right responds
A one-off classroom induction or a static poster is quickly forgotten once a worker is back on the floor under time pressure.The simulation can be repeated in five to ten minutes, so refresher training is cheap enough to actually happen.
Paper sign-off sheets show that a worker attended a session, not that they can apply the technique.Each checkpoint needs an active, scored decision, so a pass reflects demonstrated judgement and not attendance.
Supervisors have no easy way to see who is competent, only who signed a form, especially before an audit or after an incident.The Admin Command Center shows per-worker logins, attempts, time on task and best score, exportable to CSV.

How a training run works

  1. Sign in. Create a worker account and read the pre-start briefing: W A S D to walk, mouse to look, E to lift.
  2. Walk the warehouse. Move between the Loading Dock, Warehouse Floor and Racking Aisle.
  3. Clear six checkpoints. The safe-lift procedure (check the route, assess the load, choose a method, use correct technique, carry, place) became six sequential checkpoints, each with a knowledge check.
  4. Choose how to move the load. Manual lift, team lift or trolley, offered to suit the weight.
  5. See the score. The attempt, time and best score are saved to the worker’s record.

Scoring and supervisor view E9

  • 100maximum score per run. Each missed checkpoint costs 15 points, and 40 or more passes.
  • 3 rightin a row starts the bonus Golden Crate Drill.
  • 3 wrongin a row starts the Hazard-Spotting Stand-Down recovery event.
  • CSVSupervisors use the Admin Command Center to see logins, attempts, time on task and best score, and export them in one click.

Built with

Three.js with procedural geometry, pointer-lock mouse-look, and browser storage. It ships as a single self-contained file for Chrome, Edge and Firefox, with no install or server.

Who it is designed for (PACT analysis) E8

Warehouse workers

Primary users, from new starters to experienced staff. Controls are limited to mouse and keyboard, and prompts are short and plain for workers who use English as a second language.

Supervisors

Time-poor and non-technical. They need a fast answer to who is trained, so the Command Center is one summary table.

Health and safety staff

An indirect audience who receive the exported CSV record, which shaped what the Command Center captures.

What I learned, in my own words

This is the summary of my individual reflection and evaluation, which uses the stages of Gibbs’s (1988) reflective cycle. Evidence labels show where each point comes from.

Adapting to change

A plan only works if it is changed openly.
  • Week 2: the client had not replied, so I chased him until the first call and meeting were secured (E5, E6).
  • Week 4: quiz content took longer than planned, so I split the writing across two members and minuted the change (E5).
  • Week 5: the client’s scope feedback produced the replay feature (FR14). I logged it as its own milestone, M_D, so it stayed traceable (E9, E10).
  • Week 6: I moved some testing work to protect the presentation date.
  • Each change followed one pattern: record it, assign an owner, update the plan.

Looking back

The change I handled worst was the late feedback at the final presentation. The requests for a clearer selection layout and more varied scoring came when there was no time left to build them, and I had not planned a way to absorb late change.

Existing skills I used

Knowing my technical limits was as useful as my strengths.
  • My skills audit rated me 5/5 for project coordination and client-facing communication, 4/5 for written documentation and only 1 to 2 for coding and 3D (E2).
  • I built a 26-task Gantt chart with seven milestones and a critical path (E10).
  • I led the client call and demonstration, and sent written follow-ups after every contact (E6).
  • I wrote the Introduction and Requirements so that each requirement could be traced to a test (E8, E9, E14).

Looking back

I did not claim to understand the 3D work. I depended on Sparas and Namshu for realistic estimates and recorded their answers.

Approach to group work

I treated teamwork as something to design.
  • In Week 1 we agreed rules covering attendance, communication, deadlines, conflict resolution and quality assurance (E4), and each deliverable had one named owner.
  • The minutes show all four members present every week through Week 8 (E5), and no conflict needed escalating.
  • Our work followed Tuckman’s (1965) stages: a short forming phase, a performing phase once roles were clear, and quick adjourning at the presentation.

Looking back

I owned the most tasks (11, against 7 to 9 for the others, E10), which made me a bottleneck, and I handled client contact too centrally. A better approach would have shared the roles, with one person leading and one taking notes at every contact. Short mid-week check-ins would also have caught the quiz-writing delay earlier.

New skills I developed

I learned to lead a technical build without writing the code.
  • Turning a conversation into testable requirements: FR1 to FR14 and NFR1 to NFR10 (E9) became tests T1 to T21 (E14).
  • Evaluating evidence critically: reviewing every client email and log for B3 taught me that records must be checked, not just written (E13).
  • Reading a security report honestly: a prototype that works is not the same as a system that is safe for real employee data under UK GDPR (E16).
  • Planning a presentation around strengths, timing and a named fallback (E11).

Looking back

The weekly reflections show this growth, from an unprepared first call in Week 2 to a structured demonstration in Week 8.

Evaluation of outcomes and my performance

The outcomes were good in several ways, and mixed in others.
  • Delivered: all fourteen functional requirements implemented, a 24/25 client rating (E12), a published website and a zero-budget marketing plan (E15).
  • Tests: T21 (admin bypass) and T20 (offline) failed, T7 passed only in part and three tests were never run manually (E14).
  • The marketing plan’s LinkedIn page, posts and outreach emails were prepared, not carried out (E15).
  • Client requests for a better selection layout and more varied scoring were not built (E14).
  • My own records had avoidable errors: a wrong weekday, an inconsistency between the weekly minutes and the client logs, placeholder dates and blank client signatures (E5, E7).

Looking back

I was strong in leadership, planning and client communication, and adequate in quality assurance. Next time I would send the client an agenda 48 hours before every contact, keep a feedback register with the client’s exact words, owners and dates, and book a feedback session at Week 6.

Employability

I can say clearly what is incomplete, and I write it down.
  • These skills apply to junior project coordinator, business analyst, QA and IT support roles.
  • I can show a Gantt chart with dependencies, traceable requirements, client emails and meeting logs, a risk plan, and a candid evaluation of what went wrong (E10, E9, E6, E13).
  • My awareness of data protection and security (E16) is relevant to any role that handles staff or customer data.

Looking back

Working across a team of designers and developers, while knowing my own limits, is what I will bring to a workplace team.

Conclusion

Lift Right achieved its main purpose: a working, well-received prototype built to a plan. My strongest contribution was keeping the team organised and the client informed. My clearest lesson is that the quality of records, early client feedback and early testing matter as much as the product. I will apply these in future teams.
